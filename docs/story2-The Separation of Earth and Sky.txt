故事名字：The Separation of Earth and Sky

第一页对应图：purakau-01-te-kore-v2.png
Te Kore is often translated as “the void” or “nothingness,” but it does not simply mean non-existence. It can also represent unrealised potential: a state in which nothing yet has form, but everything is capable of becoming.

From Te Kore emerged the successive stages of Te Pō, the long night. Thought, consciousness, desire, energy, time, and space gradually developed. Through these stages, the possibility of the world took shape.

第二页对应图：purakau-02-ranginui-papatuanuku-v2.png
Then came Ranginui, the sky father, and Papatūānuku, the earth mother.

They lay together in a close embrace. There was no space between earth and sky and no light. Their children were born between them, confined within a cramped, damp world of darkness.

Their children were atua associated with different domains of nature, commonly including Tāne-mahuta of forests and birds, Tangaroa of the sea, Tāwhirimātea of winds and storms, Tūmatauenga of war and human activities, Rongomātāne of cultivated foods and peace, and Haumia-tiketike of wild foods.

第三页对应图：purakau-03-children-debate-v2.png
The children eventually became unable to endure the darkness and discussed how they might gain their freedom.

Tūmatauenga proposed killing their parents. His brothers rejected this plan, arguing that their parents should be separated but allowed to live.

Only Tāwhirimātea opposed the separation completely. He loved his parents and wanted them to remain together.

One by one, the other brothers attempted to force earth and sky apart, but each failed.

第四页对应图：purakau-04-tane-separates-sky-earth-v2.png
Finally, Tāne-mahuta lay with his back against Papatūānuku. Placing his shoulders against the earth, he pushed upward against Ranginui with his powerful legs.

With great pain and sorrow, earth and sky were forced apart.

For the first time, light entered the world. The children could stand, stretch, and see one another. The world moved from Te Pō, the darkness, into Te Ao Mārama, the world of light and understanding.

Ranginui and Papatūānuku continued to grieve for one another. Ranginui’s tears fell upon the earth as rain, while Papatūānuku’s sighs and tears rose as mist and dew.

第五页对应图：purakau-05-tawhirimatea-storm-v2.png
Tāwhirimātea was furious with his brothers for separating their parents. He rose into the sky to live with Ranginui and summoned his children—the winds, clouds, rain, and storms—to wage war against them.

He attacked Tāne’s forests, breaking branches and uprooting trees. He then assaulted Tangaroa’s oceans, scattering Tangaroa’s descendants. Some remained in the sea as fish, while others fled into the forests and became reptiles.

Rongo and Haumia hid within their mother Papatūānuku, who sheltered them beneath the earth.

Only Tūmatauenga stood against Tāwhirimātea. Neither could completely defeat the other, which is why storms continue to challenge humanity.

第六页对应图：purakau-06-humanity-and-nature-v2.png
Angry that his brothers had not helped him fight Tāwhirimātea, Tūmatauenga overcame their domains through his descendants, humanity.

Humans cut Tāne’s trees for buildings, tools, and canoes; caught Tangaroa’s fish; cultivated Rongo’s crops; and dug up the wild foods of Haumia.

Humanity could not defeat Tāwhirimātea, however. People may use the resources of forests, seas, and land, but they remain subject to the weather.

This expresses a central Māori idea: human beings are not separate from nature. Through whakapapa—genealogy and kinship—humans, atua, and the natural world all belong to the same extended family.

第七页对应图：purakau-07-creation-continues-v2.png
After separating earth and sky, Tāne adorned Ranginui with the sun, moon, and stars.

In some traditions, Tāne then formed the first woman, Hineahuone, from the earth and breathed life into her. She gave birth to Hinetītama, who later entered the underworld and became Hine-nui-te-pō, the guardian of death.

Creation therefore did not end with the separation of earth and sky. It continued through the emergence of life, knowledge, humanity, and death.
